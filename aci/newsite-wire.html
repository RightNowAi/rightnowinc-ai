Website project

Gathering Team Input

ACI is getting a new website. Your job is to find out what the people who talk to students every day think it needs. This guide tells you exactly how.

You report to  David Project  New website build
01

What you’re doing and why

We’re about to hire a company to build ACI a new website. Before we ask them to quote a price, we need to know everything it has to do.

Right now a lot of that knowledge is stuck in people’s heads. The admissions reps know which question comes up on every single call. The instructors know what surprises students in week one. Nobody has ever written it down.

Your job is to go get it. Talk to people, write down what they say, and hand it back in one organized list.

You are not deciding what goes on the website. You are collecting raw material so someone else can decide. That makes this easier than it sounds — you don’t have to judge whether an idea is good.

Your four steps

  1. Build your monday boardSection 07 is the exact setup. Do this first — don’t take notes somewhere else and try to move them over later.
  2. Book time with each person on the list15 minutes each. In person or a quick call is much better than email. People say more out loud than they type.
  3. Run the conversation using the scriptsSection 06. Show them the proposed site map, then ask the questions for their role.
  4. Tell David when you’re doneHe can see the board the whole time, so there’s nothing to hand over. You don’t summarize, sort, or decide anything.

06

What to say

Same opening for everyone. Then use the questions for their role.

Start every conversation like this

“ACI is building a new website. Before we hire anyone to build it,
we want to know what you think it needs — you talk to students
way more than the website does.

Here’s what we’re proposing. Take a look and tell me what’s
missing or wrong.”

Then show them the site map from Section 03 and stop talking. Let them read it.

Ask everyone

  1. What do people get wrong about ACI before they talk to us?
  2. What do you wish the website said, so you didn’t have to say it out loud every day?
  3. Looking at this list — what’s missing?

Admissions

  1. What are the three questions you answer on almost every call?
  2. What’s the thing that most often ends a conversation?
  3. What do people ask that we have no page for?
  4. What do you find yourself emailing or texting people over and over?

Instructors

  1. What surprises new students in their first two weeks?
  2. What does the lab actually look like on a normal Tuesday?
  3. What do students show up believing that turns out to be wrong?
  4. Are there students who’d be willing to tell their story?

Front desk & student services

  1. What do walk-ins ask before anything else?
  2. What do current students come to you for that the website could handle?
  3. What do parents or spouses ask when they come along?

Financial aid

  1. What part of paying for school confuses people most?
  2. What funding do people not realize they qualify for?
  3. What do people assume it costs, before you tell them?

Close every conversation with this

“Last thing — do you have any photos or videos of the labs,
students working, anything like that? Even phone pictures.”

Real photos from staff are worth more than anything we could hire someone to shoot. Ask every single time.

08

Eight rules

Paste these into a note on the board so you have them while you work.

  • Log repeats. Every time.If six people say people don’t know what it costs, that’s six separate items. The repetition is how we know it’s the biggest problem. If you combine them into one item, we lose that completely. This is the most important rule here.
  • One item per idea, not per person.If someone gives you five ideas in one conversation, that’s five items. Don’t type a whole conversation into one item.
  • Enter items the same day.Even roughly. You will forget the exact wording overnight, and the exact wording is the valuable part.
  • Use their words in What They Said.“I have to look it up while they’re on the phone” is more useful than “wants easier cost info.” Quote them.
  • Pick one topic, not several.Choose the closest fit. “Other” is a perfectly good answer. Tagging things with three topics makes the counts meaningless.
  • Ask why, once.If someone says “we need a chat box,” ask “what would that fix?” The answer is usually more useful than the idea. One follow-up is enough — don’t interrogate.
  • Don’t judge the ideas.Some will be impossible or expensive. Not your call. Log it and move on.
  • Leave Reviewed on New.That column belongs to David. Don’t change it, and don’t promise anyone their idea is getting built. If someone asks, say “I’m collecting everything and passing it along.”
09

Two things people will bring up

Someone will suggest posting salaries or job placement rates. This comes up every time. ACI is not allowed to publish those — there are rules from the Department of Education and the state about how schools describe job outcomes.

Don’t argue and don’t explain the rules. Just write it down and say: “Good one — there are some rules around how we can word that, so I’ll flag it.” Then log it like any other item and set Topic to Jobs.

Someone will want to change the site map itself. That’s fine and useful — write it down like any other idea. But don’t debate it with them, and don’t agree to change anything. Note it and move on.

10

You’re done when

  • You’ve talked to at least one person from every group in Section 05.
  • Every conversation has items on the board.
  • You’ve asked every single person about photos and videos.
  • The board is shared with David.

You do not need to summarize it, sort it, or figure out what it means. Leave the board exactly as you built it. Someone else does the next part.

If you get stuck, or someone gives you an answer you don’t know what to do with, ask David. That’s not a failure — it’s faster than guessing.
